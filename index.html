Reelio — Designing the platform the film industry has been missing

From research to high-fidelity prototype: a full solo UX project tackling the real problem of hiring freelance film crews under time pressure.

User interviews Competitor analysis Affinity mapping Atomic Research Persona & scenario Information architecture Lo-fi wireframing Usability testing ×2 Design system Hi-fi prototype WCAG 2.1 AA

The complete Reelio user journey from search to booking confirmation.

The brief

Design a platform that helps companies find and hire freelancers for film and content production — faster, safer, and with more confidence than anything currently available.

My role

Sole designer across the entire project: research, competitor analysis, persona, information architecture, wireframing, two rounds of usability testing, design system, and high-fidelity prototype.

The problem

Finding a film crew can take 50 phone calls. That's not a quirk — it's a design failure.

I started with three semi-structured interviews with people who hire freelancers for video production — from TV producers to creative directors at advertising agencies. One quote cut to the heart of it immediately:

"I call all kinds of people until I find someone. It can be 50 calls sometimes."

The problem wasn't that these professionals lacked networks. It was that their networks were invisible to any platform. They fell back on WhatsApp groups, Facebook posts, and word of mouth — not because they preferred it, but because the platforms built for this industry couldn't answer the one question that matters most: who is actually available on the dates I need?

I ran a competitor analysis across five platforms — ProductionHUB, Storyhunter, Mandy, Upwork, and others. Every single one was built around individual search. None showed availability. None showed whether two freelancers had worked together before. That gap became Reelio's entire design opportunity.

Design decisions

Six design principles, one guiding rule: reduce time to confidence

Before touching a single screen, I wrote six design principles anchored to the research. The most important: every screen had to help Sara (my persona) feel certain faster — less searching, less uncertainty, less back-and-forth before committing to a hire.

Round 1 — Lo-fi usability testing

The Reelio Network confused everyone. And that told me exactly what to fix.

Three participants completed moderated remote sessions. Three critical errors surfaced:

  • 100% of participants could not understand the Reelio Network without assistance. The concept was right — the context was absent.
  • "Booking confirmed" created false expectations. Participants believed the booking was final before the freelancer had accepted.
  • The "Book this team" button appeared before users understood who the team was — and was scrolled past without engaging.

All three still completed the search task successfully, and all three said they would use Reelio if it existed. The core concept was sound. The execution needed surgery in specific places.

What still needs iteration

Portfolio thumbnail previews in search results; a relevance ranking signal; clearer Reelio Network percentage explanation; transparent total pricing; and a short personal freelancer bio. These are the starting points for the next sprint.

What I learned

Research is only useful if it stays visible during design

Every change in the hi-fi prototype is traceable back to a specific finding. When a participant said "Hired by feels like very relevant information and should be moved up," that quote became a design requirement, not a suggestion. That traceability — from behaviour to decision — is the discipline I want to bring to every project I work on.

Yobr — Designing a mobile app that connects students to real-world work

From first prototype to A/B tested iteration: a full design arc across two modules.

The brief

Design a mobile app for Yobr — a platform that connects students with internships, projects, and companies tailored to their skills and interests.

My role

Solo designer across the full process: user flow, wireframing, high-fidelity prototype, design review, and two rounds of user testing including a direct A/B comparison.

The challenge

How do you make a job platform feel personal, not overwhelming?

I designed the full experience from splash screen to live chat with a company. The user flow covered seven key screens: Splash → Onboarding (3 screens) → Sign Up → Home → Chat. Every decision had to earn its place.

First test — key insights

What the first round of user testing taught me

After a moderated design review session using the think-aloud method, five clear insights emerged. The most actionable: the chat feature's value depended entirely on who you could contact — if Yobr itself wasn't available as a contact, the feature felt purposeless.

1
Users want to contact Yobr directly — not just companies or other students
2
Chat value was unclear without a visible support option
3
Onboarding felt smooth — but personalisation should come after sign-up
4
Simplicity and clarity rated highly — no sense of overwhelm

A/B test — Design A vs Design B

The chat redesign was the most significant change — and the most validated

Design B introduced category tabs (All / Employers / Support), role labels under each contact name, a "Yobr Support" contact, a more prominent recommended project card, card shadows on company tiles, and a background highlight on the active nav tab. I then ran a direct A/B comparison with two participants.

Design A: flat contact list — users scored understanding of contacts 3/5.

Design B: categorised tabs + role labels — scored 5/5 on both understanding and chat clarity.

Add a lightweight post-signup flow for skills and interests, preserving sign-up simplicity while improving recommendation quality.

Group project 2025 · UX Design Program — Group 10

NordicC — Making cybersecurity feel human for small business owners

A full design thinking project: from user interviews to a tested, iterated platform for non-technical SMB owners.

Interviews & research Affinity mapping Atomic Research Persona Scenario & storyboard Information architecture Wireframing Usability testing
My role

I contributed across the full process as one of three designers. My specific focus: research synthesis, persona and scenario development, wireflow design, Guided Fixes annotations, the Peace of Mind Report screens, and the usability testing report and recommendations.

Guided Fixes: problems sorted by severity in plain language — one clear action per card.

The brief

Design a cybersecurity support platform for small to medium businesses — using AI-driven features to help non-technical owners understand and respond to security risks.

The team

Joanna Jensen Sjönvall (me), Angelina Ljungberg, and Thea Traasdahl Johansen — collaborating entirely remotely across Norway and Sweden.

The problem

Small businesses aren't failing at cybersecurity because they don't care — they're failing because existing tools weren't built for them

Our research found the same thing across every interview: these owners understood the risk. They just couldn't act on it. The tools they encountered were built for IT teams. The alerts felt like spam. The dashboards were overwhelming.

"To trust a security warning, it has to feel real and important — not just noise that I end up ignoring."

We made a deliberate decision not to build a traditional security dashboard — and we could justify that choice with our research. The design response had to be built around removing friction, not adding features.

Usability testing

What we built was mostly right — and one thing was clearly wrong

Three participants, two scenarios: responding to a security notification, and opening the monthly report.

  • The "Fix it now" button worked immediately. Users understood it, trusted it, and wanted to click it.
  • Yellow was too close to "safe." Users weren't responding with the right urgency — we replaced it with orange.
  • 67% found the Peace of Mind Report unclear when nothing had changed. Reports should only appear when something meaningful has happened.

Before: original Lighthouse View — pop-up notifications, no single clear status.

After: one status, one action — no pop-ups, no noise.

Key changes from testing

Replaced yellow with orange for mid-severity. Removed the separate report notification — consolidated into a single in-app view. Simplified Guided Fixes by removing the large header. Redesigned the confirmation to a single "Problem solved" message.
